I'm 43, with a two-year-old and a baby, and I picked a goal I'm not built for, starting at 258 lb: under three hours at the Chicago Marathon on October 10, 2027. Five AI coaches plan every day of it from my watch. As of Nov 21, the watch says I'd run 4:36:10.

You're in. The first letter comes Sunday night.

Sunday nights I write down what the week really was, missteps included, with the new episode at the bottom when there is one.

Why I'm doing this

I'll be 57 when she starts high school.

My son was born a few weeks after I turned 41. My daughter came this April. By the time they're old enough to race me up a hill, I'll be in my mid-fifties, and I'd like to still win a few of those.

The other reason is quieter. I want them to grow up watching their dad go after something he wasn't built for, in the dark, on the mornings he'd rather not. Somewhere in this year they'll see me come up short. I want them to see the next morning too.

Where I've been

I've been faster.

In June 2024 I ran a half marathon in 1:32:31, on close to 80 miles a week. Runners turn a race time into one fitness score called VDOT, so a 5K and a marathon can be compared on the same scale. That half was a 49.2. My watch data puts me near 40.5 today, and sub-3 at Chicago takes about 53.5.

The road to Chicago

Slower to get faster.

The plan builds race by race. Most of my miles are easy, on Mission Trails dirt and up hills, so the hard days can be hard. The miles climb from 32 a week now to 90 in August, and every race resets the paces for the block after it.

Where the rules come from

The research writes the rules.

Every coach was built from published sports science: 597 peer-reviewed studies on running, strength, recovery, nutrition and the mental side, along with coaching methods that have built real marathoners. Each finding became a rule. When my data hits a rule, the coach acts on it. When new research clears the bar, Kai, Bo and Mo add it on their own.

Running · Kai

The research

The best distance runners keep at least 80% of their running easy.

The rule in my plan

Most of my miles stay easy, and the watch holds me to the easy range.

Recovery · Mo

The research

In a study of 5,205 runners, a run 10 to 30% longer than their longest of the previous 30 days was linked to about 1.6 times the injury rate.

The rule in my plan

No single run goes more than 10% past my longest of the last 30 days.

Strength · Bo

The research

In a small study of masters marathon runners, heavy lifting alone cut the energy it took to hold marathon pace by about 6%. A lighter lifting program showed no gain.

The rule in my plan

A few heavy sets of three, jumps on their own day, all of it hours after the run.

Nutrition · Juno

The research

For runs past an hour, endurance research puts fueling at about 60 grams of carbs an hour.

The rule in my plan

Long runs carry a drink at 60 grams an hour. Easy days stay lighter.

How it works

The run ends. The coaches start.

Nobody types a run in. My watch, chest strap and scale feed the coaches on their own, and the coaches change my plan on their own. The numbers on this site come off the same watch. The site rebuilds when the plan changes, and every Sunday night.

  1. It records

    The watch and a chest strap catch every lap and every heartbeat. Overnight the watch catches my sleep, and the scale catches my weight.

  2. It syncs

    Every 10 minutes the system checks for a new run and pulls it in. No upload, no typing.

  3. They decide

    Kai reads the paces and the hills, Mo the sleep and recovery. Bo fits the next lift around the run and Juno sizes the day's food. They change the plan themselves.

  4. Tomorrow lands

    The next run is on my watch with a range on every step, and by 4:50 AM the day's lift, warm-up and meals are on my phone.

What the coaches do on their own

  • Analyze every run, night of sleep and weigh-in

  • Turn a hard day easy when the data says so
  • Build tomorrow's run, lift and meals
  • Write down every decision they make

A real one from this week: Kai reset my heart-rate zones from the chest-strap data of my last race, and keeps re-figuring my easy pace from the last 28 days of runs. Nobody asked. The new ranges were on my watch the next morning.

The watch can only make a day easier. A rough night turns a hard run into an easy one. A great morning never adds reps.
