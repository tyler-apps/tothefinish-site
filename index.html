To the
finish.

Most days start at 5:45 with a banana in the dark, because Juno won't let me run on an empty stomach. I'm 43, with a two-year-old and a baby, and I picked a goal I'm not built for, starting at 258 lb: under three hours at the Chicago Marathon on October 10, 2027. This morning my watch says I'd run 4:35:18. Five AI coaches read every run I log and fight over what comes next. I might not make it. Every week goes up here anyway, the bad ones too, and if you're chasing a number of your own, you'll find yourself in here somewhere.

You're in. The first letter comes Sunday night.

Sunday nights I write down what the week really was, missteps included, with the new episode at the bottom.

Why I'm doing this

I'll be 57 when she starts high school.

My son was born a few weeks after I turned 41. My daughter came this April. By the time they're old enough to race me up a hill, I'll be in my mid-fifties, and I'd like to still win a few of those.

The other reason is quieter. I want them to grow up watching their dad go after something he wasn't built for, in the dark, on the mornings he'd rather not. Somewhere in this year they'll see me come up short. I want them to see the next morning too.

    Mo · Recovery

    My best trait, on the wrong day.

    I like to push. On a hard day that's the whole point. On an easy day it costs me. On November 11 I ran six easy miles and spent 45 minutes of them over my easy heart rate. Mo saw every one of those minutes.

    This is the fight I'm in all year: getting slower on the easy days so I can get faster on the hard ones. The best distance runners in the world keep at least 80% of their running easy. Here's how I'm doing. If you run your easy days too hard too, you'll know this chart.

    How easy were the easy days?

    Running time by lap heart rate: easy is my zone 2 or below (under 121), hard is threshold and up (152+), steady is everything between. Hills on Mission Trails push some easy miles into steady.

    Mo

    No run too far past the last long one

    A study of 5,205 runners on Garmin watches went looking for what sets up overuse injuries. Weekly mileage rules predicted nothing. A single run more than 10% longer than the longest of the month before made an injury 1.64 times more likely. So Mo watches each run, more than the week.

    Each dot is a run, in minutes (trail miles are slower, so time is the fair measure). The line is 10% past the longest run of the 30 days before it.

    Mo

    How much did I sleep?

    Sleep is the recovery tool with the best evidence behind it. With a baby in the house, it's also the one I'm shortest on. On a calorie cut, short nights shift the weight you lose from fat toward muscle, and under six hours the risk of catching a cold runs about four times higher. Mo counts hours asleep and ignores the sleep score. A short night can make a hard run easier. A long one never adds reps.

    Hours asleep each night, from my watch. The dashed line is 7 hours. Orange nights made it. Watches count a broken night long, so the faded bars sit a little lower in real life.

    Mo

    Bo · Strength

    The bar waits for the miles.

    Bo would have me in the garage every day. He doesn't get that. The run comes first, and hours later I'm under the bar for a few heavy sets of three, stopping well before it turns into a grind, with some jumps at the end. In the studies, heavy lifting plus jumps made runners faster than any other kind of strength work, while light circuits did nothing. In marathoners my age it made marathon pace about 6% cheaper to hold. Three lifting days a week now, two by summer.

    Bo

    Rook · How the coaching works

    The run ends. The coaches start.

    No one types my workouts in. I stop my watch, it syncs, and within minutes the coaches have the file. Kai takes the paces and the climbs. Mo goes straight to my heart rate on the hills. By the time I'm out of the shower, tomorrow's workout is on my wrist with a range on every step. Here's the count so far.

    1

    Record

    The watch and a chest strap catch every lap and every heartbeat.

    2

    Sync

    Checked every 10 minutes. A new run is pulled in as soon as it lands.

    3

    Read

    Kai reads the run: paces, heart rate, the hills. A slow mile up a climb gets credit for the faster flat mile it took the same effort to run. Bo, Juno and Mo check their lanes.

    4

    Decide

    The research's rules move my targets and zones, and every change gets logged.

    5

    Send

    The new workouts go to the watch, with the pace or heart-rate range on every step.

    Who made the calls

    Every call is a dated line in the coaches' logs: a pace target moved, a run rewritten, a zone reset, a plan change. Orange are Kai's automatic calls, made from my watch data with nobody asking. White are a coach's own calls. Gray are the times I overruled one.

    My heart-rate zones, set by Kai from my own race

    Threshold comes from my last race, minute 10 to the finish. Kai re-checks them after every sync and moves them only on a race or a field test. Next, the strap's beat-to-beat data will measure exactly where easy ends.

    How the coaches decide

    The research sets the rules. The watch can only slow me down.

    1. Speed first. A 5K in February, a 10K in May, a half in September, then Chicago. Miles climb to about 90 a week in August.
    2. Hills every week. Short hill sprints on Mondays and longer repeats on hard days. Most easy miles happen on Mission Trails dirt.
    3. The watch only makes a day easier. A rough night or a low recovery week turns a hard run into an easy one. A great morning never adds reps.
    4. Heavy lifting, short sessions. Legs on the hard-run days, hours after the run. Heavy sets of three with jumps, never a grind.
    5. Fuel the work. Light on easy days and carbs around the hard ones. Past an hour of running, 60 grams of carbs an hour.
